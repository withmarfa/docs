---
title: Permission bundles
description: How the consent screen groups scopes into a few plain-language permissions.
---

When an app asks to sign in, the consent screen does not show the raw OAuth scope list. It groups the requested scopes by what they let an app do, with a per-type toggle under each group. The issued token still carries the underlying scopes; the bundles are a presentation and request convenience over the [scope grammar](/api-reference/authentication#scopes).

## The default bundles

A hosted instance ships five bundles, all granted by default:

| Bundle | What granting it allows |
| --- | --- |
| Read your content | Read every core content type: notes, tasks, bookmarks, highlights, calendar, contacts and places, messages, files, media, and connected accounts. |
| Write your content | Create and change every core content type. |
| Content from your connected services | Read what your integrations have synced: Google, Raindrop, Readwise, Todoist, and captured email. |
| Things with your own custom types | Read and write types you define yourself, including ones you define later and ones under your own publisher handle. |
| Your profile | Confirm your identity and read your name and email. |

The content bundles expand to concrete, enumerated scope sets — `core.note:read`, `core.task:read`, and the rest, plus `system.connection:read` under Read. Connected-service types are read-only in the default grant: an integration row is a faithful mirror of the upstream service, and third-party writes would fork it. The bundles exclude the remaining `system.*` security internals (credentials, devices, webhooks) and the `app.*` namespace. The exact scope set each bundle expands to is advertised on the discovery document under `marfa_permission_bundles`.

## Per-type control

Every group is collapsed, and every shipped group is pre-ticked, so the common path is a single approve. Expanding a group reveals one toggle per content type: Notes, Tasks, Calendar, and so on. Unticking a type drops exactly that scope from the grant, and the issued token narrows to what stays ticked. The per-type toggles are the granular control; there is no separate raw-scope screen, and [partial grants](/api-reference/authentication#partial-grants) are honored end to end.

Because the scopes are concrete rather than wildcards, unticking Calendar removes `core.event:read` from the token and nothing else. The narrowing the user sees is exactly what the token carries.

Whether a group starts ticked is the bundle's own declaration, not a property of the screen. Each bundle carries a `default_on` flag, and a bundle that declares itself off renders offered but not granted: leaving it alone grants nothing from it, and ticking it grants exactly its scopes. Every bundle Marfa ships declares itself on, which is why the common path is unchanged; the flag exists so a bundle can carry something a person should have to reach for rather than merely leave alone. The two hidden mechanisms are the exception. `openid` and `offline_access` are submitted without a visible toggle, so a person cannot be said to have declined one, and `default_on` does not apply to them: a bundle that means to withhold a mechanism stops requesting it.

The flag governs the first offer only. On a return visit the [re-consent diff](/api-reference/authentication#consent-persistence) lists what you already allowed, and those stay ticked whatever their bundle declares, because a scope you granted before is being shown rather than offered. Unticking it there would read as a narrowing, and a narrowing revokes the app's live tokens.

## The device flow cannot offer one

The [device flow](/api-reference/authentication#device-authorization-grant) confirms a scope list rather than presenting per-scope toggles, so there is no tick available on that screen and nothing for an off-by-default bundle to mean. Rather than granting on one approval what the flag exists to withhold, device-flow initiation refuses any request that reaches a scope only an off-by-default bundle offers, not just one that names it: `core.*:read` is refused where `core.event:read` is withheld, because approving that wildcard would hand over the withheld scope on the same single click. The client gets `invalid_scope` naming one withheld scope the request reached rather than the scope it asked for, since a client told only that its own wildcard was refused learns nothing it can narrow to, and naming every scope reached would hand the whole withheld set to a caller who has not signed in.

The same reasoning shapes what a dynamic client registration receives when it names no scope: on-by-default bundles only. A client that wants an off-by-default bundle's scopes names them at registration, and then consents to them on a surface that can ask.

The screen is shown once per app and scope set. Once a user has approved, returning to the app — including after signing out and back in — goes straight through without a second prompt; the screen comes back when the app asks for something new. See [consent persistence](/api-reference/authentication#consent-persistence).

## Enforcement

The bundles are a grouping over real scopes, so they genuinely bind. An OAuth access token is enforced against exactly the scopes granted at consent, regardless of the signed-in user's role: the role is the ceiling on what an app can be granted, not a pass-through of the user's own access. Unticking Write at consent blocks the app from writing, even for a space admin. See [Permissions](/api-reference/permissions) for how granted scopes project into the permission maps.

## Custom types narrow as a unit

Types a person defines do not exist when an app builds its request, so no concrete scope list can name them. The custom-types bundle carries namespace wildcards instead — `user.*:read` and `user.*:write` always, plus the same pair for each publisher-handle namespace holding custom types **the person registered themselves**. A type that arrived with a connected service is in their space and is not part of this bundle: it belongs to the integration that published it, and the integration's own scopes are how it is reached. The identifier cannot make that distinction — a vendor's type and a person's under their own handle are the same shape — so the stored provenance does. On hosted deployments those handle namespaces are the consenting space's own, resolved when the consent screen renders: a space's registrations reach its own screen immediately, and never anyone else's. The wildcard is what the request names, the consent screen lists the custom types the space holds today beneath the toggle, and the grant matches whatever types exist in that namespace when a request is checked. Whether a namespace scope can be requested at all is enumerated at server start, so a handle namespace first used after start becomes requestable on the next restart — the same boundary the rest of the scope surface keeps. On a single-space deployment the deployment's own namespaces are folded in at start.

Two consequences follow from that shape, and the screen says both plainly. Narrowing is all-or-nothing across custom types — unticking the toggle drops all of them, and there is no per-type control inside the wildcard. And a type defined after consent is covered by the standing grant without a re-ask, unlike the core bundles, where a type added in a later release is only granted on the next connect.

The `app.*` namespace stays out of every default bundle — those are other apps' private types. An app requests its own namespace explicitly; the scope is in the allowlist even though individual runtime types are not.

## Self-hosting

The bundle set is configuration. Override it with the `MARFA_PERMISSION_BUNDLES` environment variable (a JSON array of bundles); an absent or malformed value falls back to the built-in defaults, so a bad override never strands the consent screen with no bundles at all.

Every entry needs an `id`, a `scopes` array, and a boolean `default_on`. The last is required rather than defaulted, and the reason is worth knowing: `default_on` decides whether that bundle's toggles start ticked, so an entry that omits it has not stated its grant behavior. Reading a missing field as `true` would turn an operator who meant `false` and misspelled the key into an on-by-default grant; reading it as `false` would fail quietly, producing a screen that grants nothing and looks broken. An entry that omits it, or gives it anything but `true` or `false`, makes the whole override malformed, and the defaults apply. Note that `"false"` in quotes is a string rather than a boolean, and is refused on those grounds. A rejected override logs an error naming the entries that failed, by id where they have one and by position where they do not, since the next symptom is otherwise a consent screen that quietly does not offer what you configured.

A scope may appear in more than one bundle, and one rule settles what that means everywhere: if any on-by-default bundle reaches it, the scope is on by default. Reaching is wider than naming, because a bundle may carry a wildcard: an on-by-default bundle offering `core.*:read` grants `core.event:read` already, so an off-by-default bundle naming that literal withholds nothing real. The consent screen ticks the scope, grouping it under the on-by-default bundle that names it where one does, and the device flow treats it as grantable. Only a scope that no on-by-default bundle reaches is withheld. The hidden mechanisms `openid` and `offline_access` are never withheld whichever bundle carries them, since neither has a toggle to tick. Any valid scope, including the full `*:read` / `*:write` wildcards, can be requested by a client; a wildcard renders as a single toggle covering its whole pattern, granted or dropped as a unit.
