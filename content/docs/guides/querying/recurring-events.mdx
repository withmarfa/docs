## The shape

A recurring event is stored as **one item**. It carries `recurrence`, an array of RFC 5545 property lines, alongside the `starts_at` and `ends_at` of its first occurrence.

Occurrences are never stored. Materializing them would mean choosing, at write time, how far into the future to go for a rule that may not end, and rewriting the lot whenever the rule changes. Instead they are computed at read time over a window you name.

This means a plain item read answers a different question than you might expect. `GET /items?type=core.event` returns the series once, dated to its first occurrence. To ask what is actually on a calendar, use the window read below.

## Reading a window

```bash
curl "$MARFA_API_URL/occurrences?from=2026-05-01T00:00:00Z&to=2026-05-08T00:00:00Z" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $MARFA_API_KEY"
```

`from` is inclusive, `to` is exclusive, and both are required — the window is what makes an unbounded rule finite. Each entry carries the occurrence's `starts_at` and `ends_at` plus the item it came from:

| Field | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `starts_at` | When this occurrence starts. Computed from the rule for a series; the item's own time for a single event. |
| `ends_at` | Present when the source event has an end; a series carries its duration onto every occurrence. |
| `item` | The item to show. |
| `series_id` | Present when the entry came from expanding a rule. Absent on a single event. |
| `replaces` | Present when a stored exception took the place of a computed occurrence; carries the start of the occurrence it replaced. |

Single events in the window come back alongside expanded occurrences, ordered by start time, so one read answers the whole question.

Add `type` to narrow to one event type. The read covers `core.event` and `google.calendar.event`, always within what the calling credential may read.

## Time zones

Stored times are instants. However a value happens to be written, offset-bearing or Z-suffixed, it names a point in time and carries no zone of its own.

A series also carries `timezone`, an IANA zone name, and that zone is part of what the schedule means: expansion renders the series start as its wall-clock hour in that zone, advances the rule there, and anchors each occurrence back to an instant. A weekly 09:00 meeting stays at 09:00 across a daylight-saving transition rather than drifting by an hour for half the year.

A series with no `timezone` has no wall clock to keep, so its rule advances in UTC from the stored instant.

The account's own zone is never consulted here. An account states one on its profile, and it is a default and a display preference: it answers what is on a given day for a caller that names no zone. A rule advances in the series' zone alone, so a person moving country does not reschedule their calendar and two people reading one shared series see one answer.

An event that ends somewhere else says so in `end_timezone`. A flight lands in a zone it did not depart from, and both ends are wall-clock facts a person reads off a ticket, so one field cannot answer for both. Absent means the event ends where it started. Recurrence expands in `timezone` alone; `end_timezone` never anchors a rule.

## Whole days

`all_day` marks an event that occupies whole days rather than a span of time. It is a declared fact, not an inference from whether `starts_at` happens to carry a time.

A whole day has no instant, so a reader takes the calendar date from `starts_at` rendered in the event's own `timezone` and never re-derives it in the reader's zone. Re-deriving is what puts a birthday on the wrong day for everyone west of the event.

`precision` answers a different question: how much of a start instant is actually known, for an event dated from memory or from a source that gave only a year. It narrows an instant that exists. `all_day` says there is none to narrow, and an event may carry both.

## Moved and edited occurrences

When one occurrence of a series differs from the rest, it is stored as its own item rather than as an edit to the series. Two things bind it:

- `original_starts_at` — the start of the occurrence it replaces.
- A `parent-of` edge from the series to the stored item.

In a window read that occurrence appears **once**, as the stored item, at the stored item's own times, carrying `replaces`. The computed occurrence it displaced does not appear.

Both halves are needed. The edge alone says the two are related without saying which occurrence is displaced, and `original_starts_at` alone leaves nothing linking it to a series.

## Limits

The window may span at most 400 days; a wider ask is refused with `400 validation_error` naming the limit.

A window may also hold at most 5000 occurrences in total, counted across every series in it. A window can sit inside the day limit and still cross this one, on a space holding enough events, so the refusal names the ceiling and the count it found rather than the dates.

One series may contribute at most 2000 occurrences to a window, counted against the window itself, so a long-running series is bounded by what you asked about rather than by its age. A series that cannot expand does not fail the read: a rule the server cannot read, one that floods the window, one too frequent to reach the window from its start, or one carrying an `EXRULE` line (which is refused rather than silently not applied) is reported in `series_errors` while the rest of the calendar returns.

Entries there are failures rather than rows, and one row can account for two — an unreadable line dropped from a rule is one failure, and expanding what is left then failing is another. Group on `item_id` to get the number of rows to go and look at. The count is scoped to the event types the read covered, so a request narrowed by `type`, or a credential permissioned for one event type, learns nothing about the rules it did not read: zero is the absence of a detected failure rather than a statement of health.

## What the read reports about itself

Every successful read carries a `scan` object naming the ceilings that applied and what this read spent against them. It is there on reads nowhere near a limit, deliberately — a bound that is only mentioned when it fires announces itself too late to be acted on.

Two flags appear on the envelope when a ceiling was reached:

- **`series_errors_truncated`** — the failure list was capped rather than the read refused, at `scan.max_series_errors`. `scan.series_errors` still carries the true total, so the difference is between "these are the failures" and "these are some of them". The list is a diagnostic beside the calendar and nothing in `data` depends on it, which is why capping it is not the silent trimming this page refuses elsewhere.
- **`expansion_incomplete`** — the read stopped expanding before it had walked every series, having spent `scan.max_unproductive_iterations` on expansions that returned no occurrence. `scan.series_unexpanded` says how many were left, and `data` may be missing what they held. Narrowing the window does not recover it, because the budget is spent walking rules from their own start before the window is reached; narrow by `type`, or fix the rules `series_errors` names.

**A space full of events is no longer refused a calendar.** An earlier version of this read refused outright past a scan ceiling, which could not be answered by asking for less: the passes that gather series and their exceptions cannot be windowed, since a rule written years ago produces occurrences in any window and an exception moved out of one still shadows the slot it left. The read is bounded instead, and says where it stopped.

Nothing is silently trimmed without saying so: a short list that looks complete is worse than an error, because a calendar that quietly omits next Tuesday is indistinguishable from an empty one. Every bound above is either a refusal or a flag on the response, and none of them is a quiet shortening.
