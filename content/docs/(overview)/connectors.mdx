Much of your data starts somewhere else: tasks in Todoist, issues on GitHub, articles in an RSS feed. A **connector** is a program that reads such a service, its vendor, and writes what it finds to your instance as items. It can also carry changes made in Marfa back to the vendor.

## A program you run

The server never runs a connector or calls a vendor. A connector is an ordinary program you run wherever suits you, on a schedule or all the time, and it uses the same API as any app. That keeps the server small, and your vendor credentials out of it.

A connector runs with a key made for it alone. The key's [source](/items-and-types#where-an-item-came-from) labels everything the connector writes, and with each record's `source_id`, its ID at the vendor, lets the connector update a record rather than write it twice.

What a connector brings in belongs in the [feed](/items-and-types#library-and-feed). A connector names `feed` on each write, or its key makes `feed` its default; otherwise its records land in the library.

## What the instance keeps for a connector

A connector can stop, restart or move to another computer without losing its place, because the instance keeps what it needs:

| What | What it's for |
| --- | --- |
| A registration | Names the connector, and shows its last heartbeat and how its recent runs went. |
| A state document | A JSON object the connector resumes from, such as the vendor's sync token. |
| Current problems | Messages about unresolved trouble, such as a record the vendor refused, until the connector clears them. |
| Agreements | One record per item of what the connector and the vendor last agreed about it, so a two-way connector can tell a change a person made from its own. |
| A hold | Lets one process at a time write, so two copies started by mistake don't overwrite each other. |

The state document, current problems and agreements belong to the key's **source**, not to one key. Replace a connector's key with one under the same source, and the connector picks up where it stopped.

## Webhooks from the vendor

Many vendors post a webhook when something changes, so a connector can act at once. The instance can give a connector an endpoint, a private address the vendor posts to.

The instance stores each delivery as it arrived, and **the connector does the rest**: it reads the deliveries, checks each came from the vendor, and marks them handled. [Receive vendor webhooks](/guides/connector-webhooks) shows how.

## Building one

[Build a connector](/guides/connector) walks through a connector from a template, and [Make a connector follow changes](/guides/connector-follow-changes) teaches it to archive what the vendor drops and report a failed run. The six connectors Marfa ships, listed in the [Catalog](/catalog/connectors), are working examples in [withmarfa/marfa-connectors](https://github.com/withmarfa/marfa-connectors), and [Run a connector](/guides/run-a-connector) runs any of them. A connector for a Google service signs in through a project you create yourself, as [Create a Google Cloud project for Google connectors](/guides/google-project) shows.

The rules behind this page are in the [connectors](https://github.com/withmarfa/marfa/blob/main/conformance/spec/connectors.md) and [inbound webhooks](https://github.com/withmarfa/marfa/blob/main/conformance/spec/inbound-webhooks.md) chapters of the contract.
