Your instance holds everything about you, so nothing reaches it without a credential, and a credential reaches only what it was given. A notes app can be allowed to read and write notes and nothing else, the way an app built for Apple's HealthKit asks for each kind of health data it needs. This page explains who can act on an instance, how a credential's reach is set, and how an app gets one.

## Who can act

Three kinds of caller act on an instance. The owner manages it, and hands access down to keys and apps:

| Caller | How it acts | What it reaches | What it can hand on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The owner | Signed in to the instance in a browser | Managing the instance: keys, apps, settings, maintenance. No items. | Any access, to a key or, by approving it, to an app |
| A key | Sent with each request, as `Authorization: Bearer marfa_k1_…` | What it was made with | Up to what it holds, if it may make keys |
| An app | Signs in through OAuth, and sends its access token, `marfa_at_…` | What the owner approved | Up to what it holds, if the owner let it make keys |

The machine the instance runs on has an authority of its own: the CLI's `--socket` uses it, from an account that may reach the instance's private socket, to claim the instance, make keys and recover the owner's password, as the [Quickstart](/quickstart) shows. The owner's authority manages the instance but reads and writes no items, so even the owner uses a key to work with data. No key or app ever gets the owner's authority, whatever it holds.

## Owner sign-in

Claim the instance with proof issued on its machine, as the [Quickstart](/quickstart) shows. A setup link lets you choose the owner's email and password; it does not sign you in as the owner. After the claim, sign in at the instance's `/auth/sign-in` page.

You can also add a passkey at `/auth/owner/passkeys` after signing in within the last five minutes. A passkey signs you in to the same owner account, and remains usable when password attempts are locked. Passkeys need a supported browser and the instance's secure HTTPS address, with a localhost exception for development.

The owner's sign-in list brings browsers, apps and keys together with their names and last use. Ending a browser session leaves apps and keys working. Ending an app ends its tokens and approval; its keys survive unless you also ask to revoke them. A browser session ends after a week without use, and signing out never deletes what it wrote.

## What a credential reaches

A key or an app's token is limited in two ways. Its maps say which data it reads and writes, and its permissions say which other operations it may use.

There are five maps. Each names things at a level, `read` or `write`, and anything a map doesn't name is out of reach:

| Map | What it names | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Type | Item types, by identifier or pattern | `"core.*": "write"` |
| Edge | Edge types | `"about": "read"` |
| Extension | Extension namespaces | `"com.example.reader": "write"` |
| Metadata | Whether it may register types and edge types | `"types": "write"` |
| Profile | The owner's name, email and avatar | `"email": "read"` |

The type map is the one that matters most. An item of a type the credential can't read answers `404 item_not_found`, exactly as an item that doesn't exist does, so a refusal never tells a credential what it can't see. An item it can read but not write is refused with `403 type_not_permitted`.

## Sources

Every key has a source of its own, chosen when it's made, such as `laptop` or `todoist`. Marfa stamps it on each item the key creates, so you can always tell which writer an item came from. With the item's `source_id`, the source also identifies the item at its writer, which is how a connector finds a record again and updates it instead of making a second one. No two keys in use share an own source, though a key can also claim other sources, so that several keys of one connector write as one.

The prefixes `oauth:` and `restored:` are reserved: a key cannot use or claim either. An app's records use its OAuth source; restoring them from an archive gives them a separate historical source, as [Back up and restore](/guides/back-up-and-restore#restore-an-archive) explains.

## How a key or an app gets access

You make a key as the owner, or with a key that holds the `keys.mint` permission, and name what it holds. A key made by another key can hold only what its maker holds, and a key can be set to expire. [Manage keys](/guides/manage-keys) shows how to make, narrow and revoke keys.

An app is never given a key. It registers itself with the instance and sends the owner to approve it, as when you sign in to an app with your Google account. The owner sees what the app asks for, one scope at a time, such as `core.note:read`, and the app's token holds only what the owner leaves ticked. That approval stands until the owner revokes it, or until the app goes unused for a year. Revoking it ends the app's access; keys the app made keep working unless the owner asks for them to go too. [Authenticate](/guides/authenticate) shows an app's side of it.
