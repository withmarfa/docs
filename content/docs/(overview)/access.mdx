Your instance holds everything about you, so each **credential** reaches only what it was given. A notes app can read and write notes and nothing else.

## Who can act

Three kinds of caller act on an instance:

| Caller | How it acts | What it reaches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The owner | Signs in to the instance in a browser | Managing the instance, listing item summaries, managing the trash, reading item counts and exporting data. |
| A key | Sent with each request, as `Authorization: Bearer marfa_k1_…` | What it was made with |
| An app | Signs in through OAuth, and sends its access token, `marfa_at_…` | What the owner approved |

**The owner can list item summaries, manage the trash, read item counts and export data through a browser session**, without making a key. An item summary gives its ID, name, source and creation time, but no properties, metadata, history or edges. The Items page searches names, not item content. Trash summaries also show type, version, deletion time and actor, and can move, restore or purge items. Those writes require a sign-in from the last five minutes. Both listings apply the instance's source filtering; an owner browser session still cannot open an item's content.

Other item content operations need a key or an app token. If a request sends a credential alongside the owner's browser session, that credential alone decides its access. No key or app ever gets the owner's authority, whatever it holds. [Sign in as the owner](/guides/sign-in-as-the-owner) shows how the owner signs in, with a password or a passkey.

Some owner actions need a **recent sign-in**, one from the last five minutes, such as revoking a key or an app, adding or changing a passkey, or approving an app's request to write or to hold a permission.

The computer the instance runs on has an authority of its own. The CLI uses it through a private socket, reachable only by the account running the server, to claim an instance, make the first key and recover the owner's password.

## What a credential reaches

A credential is limited in two ways. Its **maps** say which data it reads and writes, and its **permissions** say which other operations it may use.

A map names things at a level, `read` or `write`, and `write` includes reading. Anything a map doesn't name is out of reach. A key has five:

| Map | Field | What it names | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Type | `type_permissions` | Item types, by identifier or pattern | `"core.*": "write"` |
| Edge | `edge_permissions` | Edge types | `"about": "read"` |
| Extension | `extension_permissions` | Extension namespaces | `"com.example.reader": "write"` |
| Metadata | `metadata_permissions` | Registering types and edge types | `"types": "write"` |
| Profile | `profile_permissions` | The owner's name, email and avatar | `"email": "read"` |

A few rules apply to single maps:

- **The type map** matters most. Only it takes a third level, `none`, which shuts out a type its wildcard covers.
- **The metadata map** covers the definitions on your instance, not an item's tags and extensions.
- **The extension map** is a key's alone: an app reaches no extensions.
- **The profile map** can write only the owner's name and avatar, never the sign-in email.

## What a refusal reveals

An item of a type the credential **can't read** answers `404 item_not_found`, as an item that doesn't exist does, so a refusal reveals nothing. An edge from such an item, and a blob only such items name, answer as missing too, though a readable edge still names a hidden target's ID. An item the credential can read but not write is refused with `403 type_not_permitted`.

## Permissions

A permission opens a group of operations, such as reading the audit log or making keys. It opens no items: a key with every permission and no type map reads nothing, and every item operation is refused `403 type_not_permitted`.

The Catalog's [Permissions](/catalog/permissions) page lists them all.

## How a key or an app gets access

You make a key as the owner, or with a key that holds the `keys.mint` permission, and say what it holds. A key made by another key can hold only the permissions and maps its maker holds, and can't outlive its maker's expiry. [Manage keys](/guides/manage-keys) shows how.

An app is never given a key: it registers itself and sends the owner to approve it. [Authenticate](/guides/authenticate) shows the app's side.

## What the owner approves

The **Approve access** page, or the device approval page for an app with no browser, offers only what the app asks for:

- **Already allowed:** what the app held before. Approving never lowers it.
- **Your data:** **No access**, **Read only** or **Read and edit** for each item type, up to what the app asked. An app that asks for `*:read` or `*:write` can be given chosen types instead.
- **Your profile:** only when the app asks for it.
- **More control over Marfa:** each permission starts off. `instance.maintain`, `schema.write` and `items.purge` are marked because they can't be undone.

Approving with everything new off **is declining**: the app gets `access_denied` and keeps what it held. Approving a write or a permission the app doesn't hold needs a [recent sign-in](#who-can-act), so the page asks the owner to [confirm it's them](/guides/sign-in-as-the-owner#confirm-its-you), and a program without one gets `403 forbidden` and no code.

The owner can later **narrow the approved access**. Tokens and refresh chains that exceed the remaining grant stop working immediately, while covered tokens keep working. Giving the app wider access again requires fresh approval.

The approval stands until the owner revokes it, or until the app goes unused for the configured inactivity period. [Authenticate](/guides/authenticate) shows an app's side of it.
